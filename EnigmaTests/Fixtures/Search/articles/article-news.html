Harbor Council Backs Tidal Barrier Plan

By Mara Quillfeather, civic affairs reporter

The Saltmere harbor council voted seven to two on Tuesday evening to approve a movable tidal barrier across the mouth of the old fishing basin, ending four years of argument over how the low-lying waterfront should prepare for higher storm surges.

The barrier, a row of hinged steel gates that rest on the seabed and rise when a surge is forecast, is expected to cost about 84 million crowns and take three building seasons to finish. Council engineers told members that the gates would stay down for all but a handful of days each winter, so fishing boats and the summer ferry could pass as they do now.

Supporters on the council said the vote was overdue. They pointed to the flood of last February, when water reached the steps of the market hall and closed the coastal road for two days, and to a regional survey that found more than six hundred homes in the basin district would be at risk from a once-in-fifty-years storm by the middle of the century.

The two members who voted against the plan did not dispute the risk. Instead, they argued that the money would be better spent raising the sea wall along the northern promenade, which they described as cheaper to maintain and easier to extend later. One of them warned that moving parts in salt water would bring repair bills the council had not yet budgeted for.

What happens next

Construction cannot start until the national coastal agency signs off on the design, a review that usually takes between six and nine months. The council plans to hold two public meetings in the spring to show residents detailed drawings and to explain how the gates will be tested before the first full winter of operation.

Local fishing crews gave the decision a cautious welcome. A spokesperson for the harbor cooperative said members mainly wanted guarantees that the basin would not be closed during the herring season, and that they would be warned well before any test closure. The council has promised to publish a schedule of planned closures at least a month in advance.

Business owners along the quay were more divided. Several cafe owners said the barrier would finally let them buy flood insurance at a reasonable price, while a boatyard manager worried that three years of building work would scare off the visiting yachts that keep his slipway busy through the summer months.

The council expects to choose a building contractor by the end of next year, once the agency review is complete and the final cost has been checked by an independent surveyor.
